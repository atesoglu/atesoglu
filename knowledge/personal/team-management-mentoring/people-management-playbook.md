# People Management Playbook

Practical notes on becoming ready to lead, avoiding common new-manager mistakes, addressing underperformance, guiding colleagues, and hiring deliberately.

## 1. Readiness: Seven Signs You're Not Ready to Lead (Yet)

1. Optimizing for efficiency, not influence — leaders create conditions for others to succeed.
2. Addiction to instant results — leadership is a long-game role.
3. Avoiding silence — leaders are comfortable with reflection and measured responses.
4. Thinking leadership is only confidence — leaders act despite uncertainty.
5. Focusing on problems, not patterns — leaders change systems to prevent recurring problems.
6. Needing rigid structure to be productive — leaders create adaptable systems.
7. Seeing leadership as a reward — leadership is service, not status.

## 2. Seven Big Mistakes I Made as a New Manager

1. **Assuming silence meant agreement**
   - The mistake: Interpreting silence as agreement, especially across cultures.
   - Tip: Proactively seek input and provide multiple ways to give feedback.

2. **Underestimating the impact of small wins**
   - The mistake: Overlooking small accomplishments that motivate teams.
   - Tip: Recognize small victories and celebrate progress.

3. **Waiting too long to address performance issues**
   - The mistake: Hoping problems go away without timely conversations.
   - Tip: Address issues promptly with empathy and clarity.

4. **Not setting clear expectations**
   - The mistake: Assuming the team understands unspoken expectations.
   - Tip: Set clear goals and success criteria for projects.

5. **Not managing up**
   - The mistake: Failing to regularly update stakeholders and ask for support.
   - Tip: Share progress and advocate for your team's needs.

6. **Trying to solve every problem myself**
   - The mistake: Micromanaging and not empowering others.
   - Tip: Delegate and coach to help team members grow.

7. **Not setting boundaries for myself**
   - The mistake: Being available 24/7 and burning out.
   - Tip: Protect your energy and role-model healthy habits.

## 3. Handling Underperformance

Underperformance is rarely just about the employee. Often it reflects:

- Unclear expectations
- Insufficient support or resources
- Poor feedback loops
- Delayed or avoided conversations

Address the cause directly: clarify expectations, provide support, create feedback loops, and hold timely conversations before the problem affects the rest of the team.

## 4. Guiding Colleagues on Outdated Methods

Approach the situation respectfully and constructively.

- **Explain current industry standards:** Contrast the outdated method with modern practices such as Agile, Lean, or Scrum and their wide adoption.
- **Explain the "why":** Highlight how updated methodologies lead to efficiency, productivity, and better results.
- **Recommend research:** Suggest training, workshops, or reading materials to deepen understanding.
- **Offer support:** Offer to pair program or provide resources and tutorials to help the transition.
- **Frame as growth:** Emphasize that adopting new methods is an opportunity for personal and professional development.

The goal is to create a supportive environment where the colleague feels helped rather than criticized.

## 5. Hiring: Efficiency vs Relationships

One recurring tension in hiring is the balance between **speed and signal** versus **rapport and trust**. An approach can be highly effective in one dimension and less optimal in another, depending on the stage of the process.

### The Efficiency-First Approach

A pragmatic hiring style prioritizes:

- Direct and minimal communication
- Fast progression through stages
- A strong focus on qualifications and independent performance
- Limited guidance, to observe how candidates operate on their own

This works well for filtering. It respects time, avoids unnecessary ceremony, and produces a clean signal of autonomy and problem-solving ability. It is especially common in product-driven or engineering-led environments where independence is expected.

### The Trade-Off at Early Stages

Early interactions are relationship-forming. Candidates are not only being assessed; they are also assessing us.

When context and framing are kept to an absolute minimum:

- Rapport can be harder to establish.
- Psychological safety may be lower.
- Candidates may feel tested rather than welcomed.

Light framing — not answers or coaching — does not dilute the quality of the signal. It can improve candidate experience, build mutual trust earlier, and strengthen employer branding. This matters especially for senior, well-referred candidates who often expect a collaborative and transparent tone upfront.

### The Takeaway

There is no single right approach, only fit for purpose.

- **Efficiency-first styles** are powerful when the goal is fast, objective filtering.
- **Context-rich approaches** are better suited when the goal is relationship-building and long-term alignment.

Be intentional: are we optimizing for signal, for trust, or for both, and does our approach match that goal at this stage?
