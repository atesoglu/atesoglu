# Engineering Work and Leadership

Notes on engineering work, leadership growth, and how technical work is communicated.

## Typical Engineering Work

Engineering work commonly includes:

- Building, debugging, reviewing, and testing software
- Documentation, design, architecture, planning, estimation, and scoping
- Production changes, deployment, monitoring, and incident investigation
- Dependency, environment, security, privacy, and compliance maintenance
- Data and business-metric analysis
- Onboarding and coordination across teams and functions
- Answering questions from teammates, customers, users, and stakeholders

## Growing Into Leadership

Moving from individual contribution toward a lead role requires becoming a multiplier. Useful practices include:

1. Share career goals with your manager early.
2. Build technical credibility and be deliberate about how others experience working with you.
3. Become a reliable teammate.
4. Develop depth in a meaningful engineering domain.
5. Connect engineering decisions to product and business outcomes.

The measure of success gradually shifts from personal output to the success of the team and the systems around it.

## Slicing Work

Small implementation-focused work items can create a ticket factory, reduce ownership, and prescribe solutions too early. Product owners should clarify the problem, outcome, and value; engineering teams should choose the implementation, refinement approach, estimation, and appropriate level of slicing.

Use frameworks such as INVEST as guidance rather than organization-wide compliance. Coach teams experiencing real delivery problems instead of imposing one decomposition style everywhere.

## Communicating Technical Risk

Technical debt is easier to prioritize when its operational or business consequence is explicit:

- Slow queries can create churn risk.
- Missing automated tests can create brand risk.
- A single point of failure can create revenue risk.

The point is not to avoid technical language, but to connect architecture and maintenance work to the cost of inaction.
